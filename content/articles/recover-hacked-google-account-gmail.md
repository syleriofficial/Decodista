## First, identify the problem {#identify-the-problem}

Choose the situation that best describes **what you can do right now**:

- **Can't sign in, or someone changed your password?** Go to **Path A** below and open Google's [official Account Recovery page](<https://accounts.google.com/signin/recovery>). The process checks account ownership; recovery is not guaranteed.
- **Know your password, but lost your verification phone?** Go to **Path B**. Check an already configured backup code, another signed-in device, a security key or a passkey.
- **Can still sign in, but suspect someone else accessed the account?** Go to **Path C**. Review security events, protect your sign-in methods and check Gmail for hidden forwarding or filters.

**Work or school account?** Contact your organisation's administrator. Managed-account recovery may follow different policies.

A Google Account session may come from a browser or app rather than a separate physical device. Google's [hacked-account instructions](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825?hl=en>) explain why regaining access and securing an account are separate tasks.

> **DECODISTA safety note:** Never share a password, live verification code, backup code or passkey approval with a supposed recovery agent. If you receive an unexpected security-warning email, open Google's account pages yourself rather than following a suspicious link.

## Path A: you cannot sign in {#cannot-sign-in}

### Step 1: use Google's actual recovery page {#open-official-recovery}

Open **[accounts.google.com/signin/recovery](<https://accounts.google.com/signin/recovery>)** in the browser yourself. Enter the email address or other information Google asks for and follow the on-screen steps.

Google may ask about a previous password, verification method or recovery contact. The questions vary by account and risk assessment. You may not see every method in a tutorial, and there is no legitimate shortcut around ownership checks. [Google's recovery guide](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7682439?hl=en>).

### Step 2: recover from a familiar environment {#familiar-device-location}

Use a phone or computer on which you frequently sign in, in the usual browser, preferably from a place where you normally access the account. Answer as many questions as possible. If a password prompt appears, use a password you actually remember rather than inventing one.

These are **Google's own recovery tips**, not a guarantee of approval. Google explicitly says that incorrect answers do not automatically end the process and that you may try again if identity could not be verified. [Official recovery tips](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7299973?hl=en>).

### Step 3: handle an unavailable recovery email or phone {#recovery-method-unavailable}

If a recovery email is inaccessible or an old number is no longer yours, use the other options Google actually displays. **Never enter recovery codes on a third-party website.** If you still control your phone number, your mobile carrier may be able to transfer it to a replacement SIM; that is different from bypassing Google's security checks. [Google's lost-phone guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185834?hl=en>).

If no available option works, follow Google's recovery questions as far as possible. Google may not be able to verify ownership, and knowing only a username or an old password does not guarantee access. [Recovery guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7682439?hl=en>).

### Step 4: respect a security hold {#security-hold}

A message saying a recovery request is delayed or protected by a security hold is not, by itself, proof that someone stole the account. Google says it may delay a recovery claim as a safeguard while it assesses whether the request is legitimate.

Google does **not** publish one guaranteed recovery time for every situation. Follow the wait and communication instructions shown in your case. [Google explains recovery holds](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/9412469?hl=en>).

There is a separate timing distinction: Google says a change to recovery information can take **up to seven days** to take effect. That is **not** a promise that every locked account can be recovered in seven days. [Google's account-recovery guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7682439?hl=en>).

## Path B: your verification phone is missing {#lost-verification-phone}

If you know your password but cannot complete 2-Step Verification, check for a backup you **set up before losing the phone**:

- **Another signed-in phone or trusted computer:** A Google prompt or an existing trusted sign-in may let you continue.
- **Saved Google backup code:** Each code can replace one second step only for the Google Account that issued it. A code works once.
- **Registered passkey or hardware security key:** Use one that is already enrolled and still accessible.
- **Another verified phone number:** If you added one previously, Google may offer a challenge there. SMS may be limited after an unusual sign-in.

These are possible routes, **not guaranteed choices shown to every user**. If none is available, use Google's Account Recovery flow. [Google's lost-phone and 2-Step Verification guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185834?hl=en>) and [backup-code instructions](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?hl=en>).

If you lost **Google Authenticator** rather than your phone number, check whether your entries were synced before treating them as irrecoverable. DECODISTA has a separate [new-phone Authenticator guide](</articles/google-authenticator-new-phone-codes-missing>) for the distinction between sync, transfer and individual account recovery.

**If the phone was stolen:** Sign out of the missing device and change the Google Account password as Google recommends, when access is available. Use the device maker's lost-device tools for the phone itself. A Google password change does not replace carrier-level action on a stolen SIM. [Google's lost-phone guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185834?hl=en>).

## Path C: you still have access, but suspect a hack {#still-have-access}

If you are signed in on a trusted device, **keep that working session available** while you review suspicious activity and secure another sign-in method. Signing out your only working session can make recovery harder.

### Step 1: examine security alerts {#review-security-events}

Go to your **Google Account → Security & sign-in → Recent security events → Review security events**. Check for password changes, recovery-information edits and sign-ins you did not initiate.

If a security event is not yours, Google instructs you to choose **No, it wasn't me** when that option appears and follow its account-security prompts. [Official hacked-account help](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825?hl=en>).

### Step 2: review unfamiliar devices and sessions {#review-sessions}

Under **Your devices**, choose **Manage all devices**, review each entry and sign out of sessions you cannot explain.

A Google session can come from a browser, app or service; the same device can appear more than once. Avoid treating the number of sessions as proof of how many people accessed the account. [Google's device explanation](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3067630?hl=en>).

DECODISTA's [unknown Google device guide](</articles/unknown-device-google-account-session>) explains when a recent device entry might be legitimate and how to handle multiple same-name sessions.

### Step 3: replace a compromised password {#replace-compromised-password}

If someone else may be using your account, follow Google's security steps to change the Google Account password to a strong, **unique** one. Where the old password was reused, change it on those other services too.

Do **not** assume the password reset is a universal kill switch for every device, integration and verification method. Review sessions and connected apps after the change. [Google's hacked-account checklist](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825?hl=en>).

### Step 4: secure recovery details and sign-in factors {#secure-factors}

Review the recovery email and phone, 2-Step Verification methods, registered security keys, passkeys and backup codes. Remove methods you do not recognise, while retaining an independent way to sign in.

If a lost phone or suspicious device has a passkey, follow Google's passkey-management route instead of assuming a password change removes it. DECODISTA covers the distinctions in [Passkeys vs 2-Step Verification](</articles/google-passkey-vs-2-step-verification>).

## Check Gmail for hidden access after recovery {#gmail-security-checks}

An account can look normal after its password is reset while Gmail settings still deserve review.

### Forwarding and filters {#forwarding-filters}

In Gmail on a computer, open **Settings → See all settings**, then check two places:

- **Forwarding and POP/IMAP:** Look for a forwarding address you do not recognise.
- **Filters and Blocked Addresses:** Look for rules you did not create that forward, archive or delete messages.

Google says automatic forwarding can send new messages to a verified destination, and filters can forward selected messages. Investigate unfamiliar rules, but do not assume a rule you knowingly configured proves a hack. [Gmail forwarding](<https://support.google.com/mail/answer/10957?hl=en>) and [Gmail filters](<https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6579?hl=en>).

If important email appears missing, review Spam, Trash, All Mail and your relevant clients as well. A missing message by itself does not prove an intruder deleted it.

### Connected apps and sensitive services {#connected-apps}

Review **third-party connections** in your Google Account. Removing an app's authorised access is not the same action as signing out a device or changing a password. Unfamiliar access deserves attention; legitimate services may need reauthorisation afterward. [Google's linked-app guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13533235?hl=en>).

If you notice unexpected purchases, payment methods or access to another account that uses Gmail for recovery, check those services directly too. Google lists payment and related-account changes among signs to investigate in a hacked-account review. [Official guidance](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825?hl=en>).

## Watch for fake “Gmail recovery experts” {#avoid-recovery-scams}

Google says it does **not** work with account/password recovery services and that you cannot call Google for consumer account sign-in help. Anyone asking for a one-time code, a backup code, remote-control access or money to unlock your account is not an alternative recovery route. [Google Account Recovery](<https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7682439?hl=en>).

Be suspicious of:
- Paid promises of guaranteed or instant recovery.
- Requests to screenshot or forward a Google verification code.
- Instructions to install remote-access software to “verify” account ownership.
- A link that resembles `accounts.google.com` but belongs to a different website.
- People claiming that a temporary security hold can always be bypassed.

For red flags involving callers and remote access, use DECODISTA's [fake tech-support scam guide](</articles/fake-tech-support-scams-remote-access-verification-code>).

## Final account-security checklist {#recovery-checklist}

Once you are safely signed in, this is DECODISTA's **recommended checklist**, not a claim that our team has inspected the account:

1. Verify ownership and sign in through Google's official account pages.
2. Review recent security events and respond to activity that was not yours.
3. Replace any compromised or reused password.
4. Sign out unknown devices and sessions, keeping a safe recovery method available.
5. Review recovery email, recovery phone, 2-Step Verification and passkeys.
6. Check Gmail forwarding, filters and unexpected mailbox rules.
7. Review third-party app access and payment information.
8. Save appropriate backup methods somewhere you can reach without a single phone.
9. Notify affected contacts or services if you have evidence of impersonation or other misuse.
10. Recheck the account later for unfamiliar activity rather than assuming one password change settled every risk.

You can run [Google's Security Checkup](<https://myaccount.google.com/security-checkup>) for its current recommendations. It cannot guarantee that no information was previously seen or copied by someone else.

**Bottom line:** Account recovery and account security are separate stages. Get back in through Google's own process, then verify devices, mail settings, connected apps and recovery methods. No article or paid service can guarantee that Google will restore a particular account.
