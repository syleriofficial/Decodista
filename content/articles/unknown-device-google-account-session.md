## What the device list may be telling you

Google shows current and recent devices, plus sessions that may not be separate hardware. These explanations are possibilities, not proof of safety. [1]

| What you see | Possible explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Same phone name, multiple sessions | One device can have separate browser, app, service or private-window sessions |
| Recent activity when you were not actively using it | Gmail, Calendar or another connected app may have communicated with Google in the background |
| Nearby but incorrect city | Google’s displayed location can be approximate |
| Old phone still listed after factory reset | Google says a recently reset device can still appear |
| Different browser | A separate browser session may be legitimate |
| Completely unfamiliar device plus an unknown security event | Treat it as potentially suspicious and investigate |

## Device vs session: what Google is actually showing you

A device is physical hardware. A Google Account **session** is a signed-in period through a browser, app or service. A new device, password re-entry, another browser or app, app authorization, or private window can create another session. [1]

Same-name entries can come from one device **or** several. If ownership is unclear, Google recommends signing out all sessions with that name. [1]

## Why one phone or computer can show multiple sessions

One laptop using Chrome, Gmail and a private window can produce separate sessions. Counted entries therefore do not reveal the number of physical devices. [1]

## Why the “last active” time can look wrong

The time is the last communication with Google—not necessarily a manual login. Background syncing from Gmail, Calendar or another connected app can make activity look newer than remembered. [1]

## Why the location may not match exactly

Google can show a nearby place or somewhere briefly visited, such as an airport. It is not exact GPS evidence, and a nearby-city mismatch does not prove safety. [1]

## Why an old or factory-reset device can still appear

Google says recently reset devices can remain among entries used within the last few weeks. It gives no exact disappearance deadline; sign out hardware no longer controlled. [1]

## When should you actually worry?

Conflicts with known devices, locations and security history raise the concern.

| Sign | Concern level |
| --- | --- |
| Multiple sessions under a normal device name | Often explainable, but verify the details |
| Nearby location mismatch only | May be benign; verify rather than assuming |
| A browser the user recently used | May be benign |
| Unknown device or session that cannot be explained | Concerning |
| Unknown Recent security event | High concern |
| Recovery phone or email changed without permission | High concern |
| Password changed without permission | High concern |
| Gmail settings or other account settings altered without permission | High concern |

A benign possibility should not overrule unfamiliar security-setting, device or product changes. [2]

## How to sign out an unknown device or session

Use Google’s current device-management path: [1]

1. Open the **Google Account**.
2. Select **Security & sign-in**.
3. Under **Your devices**, choose **Manage all devices**.
4. Open the device or session.
5. Select **Sign out**.

If ownership of same-name sessions is unclear, sign them all out. A legitimate device can sign in again later. [1]

## Check Recent security events next

Open **Google Account → Security & sign-in → Recent security events → Review security events**. For an event that was not yours, select **No, it wasn’t me** and follow Google’s security steps. Check unfamiliar sign-ins, recovery or password changes, 2-Step Verification changes, and Gmail filters or forwarding rules you did not create. [2]

## Should you change your Google password?

If the entry has a verified ordinary explanation and no suspicious event, an emergency change may not be necessary. If another person may be using the account, Google says to change the password immediately, use one not previously used for that account, and change reused passwords on affected services. Then review devices, recovery information, verification methods, passkeys and linked apps. [2][3]

## Why changing your password may not remove every type of access

Google says changing or resetting the password signs the account out broadly, **except** for identity-verification devices, some devices with third-party apps that have account access, and certain helpful home devices with account access. It is not a universal logout; review devices and third-party connections separately. [3]

## Device/session sign-out is not the same as revoking an app

Signing out addresses account access on a device. Removing a third-party connection controls the app’s authorized relationship with the account. Google separates **Sign in with Google**, access to Google Account data and other linked-app relationships. Neither action substitutes for the other, so [review third-party Google Account access](</articles/remove-third-party-google-access-without-losing-login>) separately. [4]

## What if the device is lost or stolen?

Open **Your devices**, select the lost device and sign it out. Supported phones, tablets or Chromebooks may offer find, ring or secure actions. Windows, Mac and Linux computers are not under **Find your phone**; Google instead directs users to change the account password. Consider changing passwords saved on the lost device. [5]

## What about a passkey on the lost device?

From another available device, sign in and remove the passkey associated with the lost device. Depending on prior setup, another signed-in phone, verified number, backup code, hardware key or passkey may provide access; not every option appears for every user. DECODISTA explains [removing a passkey from a lost device](</articles/google-passkey-vs-2-step-verification>) and why recovery routes still matter. [6]

## If you cannot sign in

Use Google’s account-recovery flow. If no other second step is available, Google says ownership review can take **3–5 business days**—an estimate, not a recovery guarantee. Work or school users may need their administrator. [6]

## Fake security alerts and support scams

Verify alerts through the Google Account directly. Never give an unsolicited caller or sender a password, code, passkey approval or remote access. See DECODISTA’s guide to [fake support and verification-code scams](</articles/fake-tech-support-scams-remote-access-verification-code>); Google also warns it does not work with services claiming to provide account or password support.

## Security checklist after a genuinely unfamiliar session

1. Sign out the unfamiliar device or session.
2. Review **Recent security events**.
3. Use **No, it wasn’t me** and follow Google’s Secure Account prompts where applicable.
4. Change the Google Account password if compromise is suspected.
5. Review recovery phone, recovery email and critical security settings.
6. Review 2-Step Verification methods and passkeys.
7. Remove any passkey tied to a lost or stolen device.
8. Review third-party Google Account connections separately.
9. Check Gmail filters, forwarding and other Google-product settings for unauthorized changes.
10. Change passwords on other services where the compromised password was reused.

Available options depend on the account and recovery methods already configured. [1–6]

## Which response fits the situation?

| Situation | Best next step |
| --- | --- |
| Same familiar device with an extra session | Inspect the details; sign out same-name sessions if unsure |
| Nearby city but otherwise familiar | Verify the activity before assuming compromise |
| Old device no longer owned | Sign it out |
| Completely unknown device or session | Sign out and review Recent security events |
| Google confirms activity was not yours | Follow the Secure Account flow and change the password |
| Lost or stolen device | Sign out remotely and remove its passkey if applicable |
| Suspicious connected app | Review or revoke third-party access separately |

## FAQ

### Why does my Google Account show an unknown device?

It may be a previous or reset device, or a browser, app or service session. If unexplained, sign it out and review security events. [1][2]

### What is a Google Account session?

A signed-in period from a browser, app or service. It is not necessarily a separate physical device. [1]

### Can one phone show multiple sessions?

Yes. Browsers, apps, services, private windows, password re-entry or app authorization can create separate sessions. [1]

### Why does Google show the wrong last-active time?

It records communication with Google, including background syncing—not necessarily a manual login. [1]

### Why is the location different from where I was?

Google may show a nearby place or somewhere briefly visited. Verify the activity; neither possibility proves safety. [1]

### Why does a factory-reset device still appear?

Google says reset devices can remain among entries used within the last few weeks. Sign it out if no longer controlled. [1]

### How do I know whether an unknown device is a hacker?

The name alone cannot establish that. Compare its details with security events and unauthorized changes; treat an unexplained entry as potentially suspicious. [1][2]

### How do I sign an unknown device out?

Use **Security & sign-in → Your devices → Manage all devices**, select it and choose **Sign out**. [1]

### Should I change my password after seeing an unknown session?

Change it if another person may be using the account. A verified benign session does not automatically require it. [2][3]

### Does changing my Google password sign out every device?

No. Google lists identity-verification devices, some third-party-app devices and certain helpful home devices as exceptions. [3]

### Does signing out a device remove third-party app access?

No guarantee. A device session and an app’s authorized connection are separate controls. [4]

### What should I do if the device is lost or stolen?

Sign it out, use supported find or lock options, remove its passkey and consider changing exposed passwords. [5][6]

### What if the lost device has a passkey?

Sign in elsewhere, remove that passkey and use an alternate method configured before the loss. [6]

### Does 2-Step Verification guarantee an unknown session is safe?

No. It adds protection but does not prove an unexplained session is safe. Investigate the entry.
